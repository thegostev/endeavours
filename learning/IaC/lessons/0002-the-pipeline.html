Warm-up from lesson 0001

Where you are

The big idea

The pipeline - the one thing
Every change takes the same checked road to production - instead of each person's own way.
In a paragraph
A pipeline is a fixed series of stages. Each stage takes the same artifact and adds proof: it builds, it passes fast tests, it runs in staging. Early stages are fast and catch most problems. Later stages are slower and more thorough. A change that fails a stage stops there. A change that passes every stage is ready for production.
Picture
Airport security. Every bag goes through the same belt, the same scanner, the same checks, whoever owns it. No bag takes a side door because its owner is in a hurry.
Where the picture stops working
At an airport, every bag gets the same check forever. A good pipeline changes: when a bug gets through, the team adds a check, so that kind of bug cannot get through again. And the bag is never changed by the checks - just like the artifact.

Words you need

pipeline
A fixed series of automatic stages that every change goes through on its way to production. Also called a deployment pipeline or a delivery pipeline.
stage
One step of a pipeline. It runs a check (build, test, deploy to staging) and passes the artifact on - or stops it.
main line
The one shared branch of the repo that the team keeps working (in Git: main). Also called trunk or mainline.
trunk-based development
Everyone works on the one shared branch, the trunk, and avoids long-lived branches. Branches that do exist live a day or two, only for review and the CI check.
feature flag
The "switch" from lesson 0001, under its usual name (also feature toggle). It lets code sit in trunk - and even in production - switched off.
CI (Continuous Integration)
Everyone merges small changes into the main line at least daily, and each merge starts an automatic build with tests.
Continuous Delivery
Every change that passes the pipeline is ready to go to production at any moment. A person decides when, with one button.
Continuous Deployment
Every change that passes the pipeline goes to production automatically. No button.
promotion
Moving the same artifact to the next environment after it passed the stage before.
blue-green deployment
Two full copies of production. The new version goes to the idle copy, then all users switch to it at once.
canary
The new version gets a small share of users first, then more as confidence grows.
rollback
Going back to the last good version (from lesson 0001: a deployment of an older artifact).

Word flags: "CI/CD" mixes all three ideas - say which one you mean; "CD" alone is not used. From this lesson on, the course says "feature flag", not "switch".

The pipeline, on one screen

Follow 1.4.2 down the road

Moment 1 - three weeks on a branch

Ana and Ben both change the API. Each works on a private branch for three weeks. On Friday they merge both branches into main. Nothing works, and nobody knows which change broke it.

Reveal

They would merge small pieces into main at least once a day, and every merge would start a build with tests. A clash between Ana's and Ben's code shows up on the day they write it, while it is small and fresh.

Why: integration bugs are cheap when they are young. Fowler's first CI article says that with CI, most integration bugs show up the same day they are made, instead of weeks later. The longer two branches stay apart, the bigger the merge and the harder the hunt.

Moment 2 - half-finished work

Ana's next feature, "shared notebooks", needs two weeks of work. CI says: merge into trunk at least once a day. But for two weeks the feature is half done, and trunk is what goes to production.

Reveal

Behind a feature flag that is off in production. Her code merges daily, gets built, tested, even deployed - but stays dark until the flag goes on. Her branches live a day, only for review and the CI check.

Why: this is trunk-based development, the practical form of CI. The Trunk Based Development site calls it a key enabler of CI: every team member commits to trunk at least once every 24 hours. The flag moves "unfinished" from a branch into the code. Two costs come with it: CI should test the flag combinations you expect (on and off), and every flag must be removed once its state is settled - or flags pile up as debt.

Moment 3 - the CI stage passes

Ana merges a small change. The CI stage builds notekeeper-api:1.4.2 and its fast tests pass. The team works with Continuous Delivery.

Reveal

Not by itself. With Continuous Delivery, 1.4.2 goes on through the staging stage. If it passes, it is ready - a person presses the button when the business wants it. With Continuous Deployment, it would go to production automatically after the last stage.

Why: both ways need the same automatic checks. The only difference is who decides the last step. Jez Humble, who named Continuous Delivery, describes Continuous Deployment as releasing every good build to users. So Continuous Deployment always includes Continuous Delivery, but not the other way round.

Moment 4 - from staging to production

1.4.2 passed every test in staging. The team presses the button.

Reveal

The same 1.4.2. This is promotion: the artifact moves on, and nothing is built again.

Why: each stage adds proof about one exact artifact. A new build for production would throw that proof away - you would ship something no stage ever checked. (Lesson 0001, moment 3: one artifact, config per environment.)

Moment 5 - a risky change

1.4.2 changes how notes are saved. The team wants real users to try it, but if it is bad, only a few users should see it.

Reveal

Canary. A small share of users gets 1.4.2. If the numbers look good, the share grows. Blue-green moves everyone at once - but it can move everyone back in one step.

Why: both strategies keep the old version ready, so going back is fast. They trade different things: blue-green needs a second full copy of production for a short time, canary needs a way to split traffic and good measurements. Neither one needs a new build to go back.

Moment 6 - fast or safe?

Another team deploys once a month, in one big batch. Their lead says: "Slow is safe. Every deployment is a risk, so we do fewer."

Reveal

Not by DORA's research. DORA measures teams with speed metrics (how often they deploy, how long a commit takes to reach production) and stability metrics (how many deployments fail, how fast they recover). The best teams score high on both at once.

Why: a monthly batch holds a month of changes. When it fails, the cause is hard to find and the rollback takes many good changes with it. Small, frequent deployments are each small, easy to check and easy to undo. The pipeline is what makes frequent deployments safe.

The four numbers DORA uses

NumberWhat it asksSpeed or stability
Deployment frequencyHow often do you deploy to production?Speed
Change lead timeHow long from commit to running in production?Speed
Change fail rateWhat share of deployments need a fix or a rollback right away?Stability
Failed deployment recovery timeWhen a deployment fails, how fast are you back?Stability

DORA added a fifth number in 2024, deployment rework rate: the share of deployments that are unplanned, made because of a production incident. Source: dora.dev.

Three kinds of feature flag

When can a flag change? It depends on where its value is read. This is lesson 0001's line between artifact and config, again.

KindIts value is setNotekeeper exampleCatch
Build flagat build time - decides what goes into the artifactleave the notebooks code out of 1.5.0A capability left out at build time cannot be turned on later
Launch flagwhen the app starts (config)NOTEBOOKS=on in staging's configA change needs a restart

Plain reading of Trunk Based Development: Feature Flags. The same page warns against scattering if/else checks through the code: put the choice behind one abstraction and pick the implementation at start-up. That idea has its own name, branch by abstraction - not taught in this course.

Blue-green and canary

Say the difference - the same words in every lesson

CI vs Continuous Delivery vs Continuous Deployment

CI: merge small changes daily, each checked automatically. Continuous Delivery: every good change is ready for production, a person presses the button. Continuous Deployment: every good change goes to production, no button.

Each one needs the one before it. Continuous Deployment always includes Continuous Delivery.

Feature branch vs feature flag

A long-lived feature branch hides unfinished work outside trunk. A feature flag hides it inside trunk, switched off.

Trunk-based development picks the flag: the code merges daily, so CI checks it daily.

Canary (deployment) vs feature flag (release)

A canary sends some users to a different running version. A feature flag keeps one running version and turns a feature on for some users.

Canary works at deployment level (Layer 2). A flag works at release level (lesson 0001).

Blue-green vs canary

Blue-green moves all users at once, and back at once. Canary moves users a little at a time.

Build vs deploy vs release (from 0001)

Build makes the thing. Deploy puts the thing in a place. Release lets users use it.

The pipeline automates the first two. The release can stay a separate decision.

Cards for your deck

Someone says "we do CD". What do you ask first?

"Continuous Delivery or Continuous Deployment - is there a button before production?"

Your team runs a CI server on every feature branch, but merges to main once a month. Is that CI?

No. CI means merging into the shared main line at least daily. A build server alone is not CI.

Staging passed. A colleague wants to "make a clean build for production". What is the risk?

Production gets an artifact no stage ever checked. Promote the same artifact.

A canary at 5% shows more errors than the old version. What do you do?

Send the 5% back to the old version. No new build is needed - the old one is still running.

Why can frequent deployments be safer than rare ones?

Each one is small, easy to check and easy to undo. DORA finds the best teams fast and stable at once.

Where do the fast tests go, and why?

In the early stages: they catch most problems in minutes. Slow, thorough tests come later.

Why does trunk-based development need feature flags?

So half-finished work can merge into trunk daily and stay off in production until it is ready.

A team has 140 feature flags, many from last year. What went wrong?

Nobody removed them. Each is debt: dead code paths and more combinations to test. Give every flag a review-for-delete date.
